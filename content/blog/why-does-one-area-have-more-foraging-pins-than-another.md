# Why does one area have more foraging pins than another?

You are choosing somewhere to walk. One neighborhood has plenty of mapped trees; another has few. Before choosing the busier patch, consider how those records reached the map. More pins can reflect more mapping effort, rather than more food to find. That follows from the mix of community contributions and institutional tree inventories described by [Falling Fruit, Forage Around’s location-data source](https://fallingfruit.org/datasets).

## What a cluster of pins tells you

Falling Fruit brings together maps made by foragers and tree inventories compiled by cities, universities, and other institutions. These inventories document trees for their care; Falling Fruit extracts food-producing species for its map. A place with an imported inventory can therefore have records that another place lacks. [Falling Fruit’s dataset directory](https://fallingfruit.org/datasets) includes both a university campus inventory in California and a community apple-tree map in Glasgow.

The useful inference is modest: pin density reflects the available records as well as the plants. A sparse area does not establish that there is nothing to forage. A dense area does not establish that it offers a better harvest. Neither conclusion follows from a count of mapped locations alone. ([How the records are gathered](https://fallingfruit.org/datasets))

## Use coverage to plan a look

When comparing places, use the pins to choose individual locations to investigate. Keep a lightly mapped area in consideration if you already have another reason to walk there. Avoid making a long trip depend only on how busy the map looks.

Keep the information sources separate, too. Forage Around credits Falling Fruit for locations, Wikipedia for reference material, and OpenStreetMap for the base map. Reference information is not verification of the plant at a pin, and a mapped location does not establish picking permission or ripeness in your region. ([Data sources and limitations](https://foragearound.com/about))

The map can help you decide where to look. Use its coverage as a starting point for a walk, rather than a ranking of which neighborhood has the most to gather.
