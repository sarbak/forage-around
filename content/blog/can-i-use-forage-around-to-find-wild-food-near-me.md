# Can I use Forage Around to find wild food near me?

Yes—but use Forage Around as a **starting point for planning**, not as proof that a plant is edible, available, ripe, or legal to pick. The map shows crowd-sourced locations of fruit and other plants, with seasonal notes and ideas for using them. Before harvesting, confirm the plant’s identity, check local rules, and get permission where needed. ([foragearound.com](https://foragearound.com/about))

## What Forage Around can show you

Forage Around provides a live map of mapped forage locations. Individual pages may include:

- The mapped plant or tree
- Approximate coordinates
- A seasonal window and peak period
- Which part is typically used
- Food or preservation ideas
- Walking directions to the location

For example, a grape listing includes a location, an August–October ripening window, and a reminder to taste before picking. ([foragearound.com](https://foragearound.com/tree/425))

The underlying locations come from Falling Fruit, a volunteer-run, crowd-sourced map. Forage Around says those locations are provided as-is and should be treated as a starting point rather than a guarantee. Plant descriptions and photos come from Wikipedia and Wikimedia Commons, while the base map uses OpenStreetMap. ([foragearound.com](https://foragearound.com/about))

## What the map cannot confirm

A mapped location does **not** establish that:

- The plant is still present
- The plant has been identified correctly
- The fruit is ripe today
- The location is free from pesticides, pollution, or other contaminants
- You have permission to enter or harvest
- Local laws allow collection
- The plant is safe for you to eat

Season windows are useful for planning, but actual ripeness varies with weather, cultivar, microclimate, and maintenance. Check the plant in person rather than relying on the calendar alone.

## A safer way to use a mapped location

1. **Identify the plant independently.** Do not eat anything based only on a map label or photograph. Poison Control advises eating wild plants only when you know with certainty what they are; mistaken identification can be fatal. ([poison.org](https://www.poison.org/articles/plant?utm_source=openai))  
2. **Ask before picking.** A tree near a sidewalk may be in someone’s yard or on private property. Do not cross fences, enter private land, or reach over a fence without permission.  
3. **Check the land manager’s rules.** Rules differ between private property, city parks, state land, national forests, and national parks. The National Park Service generally prohibits removing plants unless collection is specifically authorized, while some other public lands allow limited personal-use gathering under local conditions. ([nps.gov](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/policy/mp-8-use-of-parks.htm?utm_source=openai))  
4. **Avoid roadsides and contaminated areas.** Traffic, herbicides, and polluted soil can make otherwise edible plants unsafe. The Forest Service recommends avoiding roadside harvesting and gathering only with the landowner’s or land manager’s permission. ([research.fs.usda.gov](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/27281.pdf?utm_source=openai))  
5. **Take only what you need.** Leave enough for wildlife, future growth, and other people. Avoid damaging the plant or disturbing the surrounding habitat.  
6. **Treat wild mushrooms as a separate high-risk category.** The CDC advises that wild mushrooms should not be eaten unless an expert has identified them; poisonous species can resemble edible ones and may cause severe illness or death. ([cdc.gov](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/wr/mm7010a1.htm?utm_source=openai))

Forage Around is most useful when it helps you decide **where to look and what questions to ask next**. The final decisions—identification, permission, legality, safety, and whether anything is ready to harvest—still belong to the person in the field.
