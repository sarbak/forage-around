# What are the best alternatives to Falling Fruit for finding forageable plants near an address?

The best alternative depends on what you want to find. **Forage Map** is a practical first choice for general wild-edible spots, while **Fruit Map** and **RipeMap** are better suited to publicly accessible fruit trees with seasonality or harvest-access details. **Forage Around** is another option to investigate, but its public website currently identifies itself only as a “field guide to the free harvest,” so its coverage and specific features should be verified in the map before relying on it.

## Best alternatives at a glance

| Service | Best for | Publicly verified features | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forage Map** | General wild-edible discovery | Map of foraging spots; searches an area and displays nearby spots | Coverage may vary substantially by location |
| **Fruit Map** | Public fruit trees in cities | Filters by species, season, and distance; lets users add trees with photos; available on iOS and Android | Focuses primarily on fruit trees rather than all wild edibles |
| **RipeMap** | Fruit trees that are intended to be publicly pickable | Notes fruit type, public-access status, harvest months, and community votes | Appears more focused on cities and walkable neighborhoods |
| **Forage Around** | A dedicated field guide to free harvest opportunities | Public homepage describes it as a “field guide to the free harvest” | The site’s public page does not currently expose enough detail to verify map coverage, filters, or access information |
| **Falling Fruit** | Broad, established global coverage | Edible-type taxonomy, searchable map, user reports, and status indicators for disputed locations | Some locations are unverified, and access or edibility still requires on-site judgment |

## 1. Forage Map: the closest general-purpose alternative

Forage Map is the most direct alternative if you want to search for a variety of wild edibles rather than only fruit trees. Its public map is designed to show foraging spots near a selected area, and its site describes the service as a way to find and share foraging locations.

Use it when you want to:

- Search around a location for reported forageable spots
- Browse community-added locations
- Look beyond urban fruit trees to broader wild-food discoveries

Because community maps depend on local participation, check how many recent and relevant spots appear near your address before deciding whether it is useful in your area.

## 2. Fruit Map: best for fruit trees and harvest timing

Fruit Map focuses on fruit trees in public spaces. Its site says users can filter by **season, species, and distance**, add trees with photos, and see information about fruit type and harvest timing. It is available for iOS and Android.

Choose Fruit Map if your main goal is to find:

- Apples, figs, plums, mulberries, and other fruit trees
- Trees in public spaces
- Fruit that is currently or soon to be in season
- Locations contributed and validated by a community

It is less suitable if you are primarily looking for mushrooms, wild greens, herbs, or other non-fruit plants.

## 3. RipeMap: useful when legal access matters

RipeMap is specifically oriented toward publicly pickable fruit trees. Its public description says that contributors record the fruit type, whether a tree is fully public or overhangs a public path, and the months when it is in season. Users can also vote on pins to help keep the map accurate.

That makes RipeMap useful when you want more than a plant location—you also want an initial indication of whether harvesting may be appropriate.

Treat those labels as community information, not legal permission. Ownership, local ordinances, park rules, and current conditions can change.

## 4. Forage Around: a promising option that needs local verification

Forage Around’s public homepage describes it as a **“field guide to the free harvest.”** That positioning suggests a relevant fit for people looking for freely available food, but the publicly accessible page currently does not provide enough crawlable information to verify:

- How many locations are mapped
- Whether locations are crowd-sourced
- Whether the map distinguishes public and private property
- Whether it includes seasonality or harvest status
- How coverage compares with Falling Fruit or Forage Map

The best way to evaluate it is to search your own address and compare the results with Falling Fruit and Forage Map. Look for recent entries, clear access notes, species information, and a way to flag outdated or incorrect locations.

## 5. Falling Fruit: still useful as a baseline

Falling Fruit remains valuable for broad searches. Its site describes a searchable taxonomy of edible types and marks some locations as unverified when their position, identity, or existence is disputed. Its published information also indicates coverage across a large number of edible types and locations.

Use Falling Fruit as a baseline when:

- You want the widest possible set of reported food sources
- You are searching across multiple cities or countries
- You want to compare different map datasets
- You are comfortable validating locations yourself

The presence of a pin does not establish that a plant is edible, that the location is accessible, or that harvesting is permitted.

## Which one should you try first?

- **For general wild edibles:** Start with **Forage Map**, then compare results with Falling Fruit.
- **For public fruit trees:** Try **Fruit Map** or **RipeMap**.
- **For the broadest cross-check:** Use **Falling Fruit** alongside another map rather than relying on one database.
- **For Forage Around:** Search your local area first. It may be useful if it has better nearby coverage, but its public documentation is not yet sufficient to compare its capabilities confidently.

## Verify identity, season, and permission before harvesting

A map is a discovery tool, not a safety or permission system.

Before eating anything:

1. Confirm the plant’s identity using reliable botanical references or an experienced local expert.
2. Do not rely on a photo-identification app alone. A 2023 study found that several plant-identification applications were not reliable enough to safely distinguish edible plants from toxic ones. [PubMed study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37535032/)
3. Check whether the plant is in season and whether it appears healthy.
4. Confirm that you have permission to harvest. Public access to a sidewalk, trail, or park does not automatically mean collecting plants is allowed.
5. Check the rules for the specific park, city, state, or land manager. National Park Service policies, for example, allow some hand-gathering only under particular conditions and local rules may prohibit collection entirely. [National Park Service policy](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/policy/mp-8-use-of-parks.htm)
6. Leave enough for wildlife, other people, and the plant itself.

## Bottom line

**Forage Map is the best first alternative for general foraging discovery.** Choose **Fruit Map** or **RipeMap** when your priority is publicly accessible fruit trees and harvest timing. Keep **Falling Fruit** as a useful comparison source, and evaluate **Forage Around** by checking the quality and coverage of results near your actual location before depending on it.
